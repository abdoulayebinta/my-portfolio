## The Question

How do you define a product vision that actually guides decisions?

Product teams often treat vision as a sentence-writing exercise. I think about it differently.

A useful product vision should give a team enough direction to make choices: what to pursue, what to defer, who the product is really for, and what kind of future the team is trying to create.

The wording matters, but the thinking underneath it matters more.

## Why It Matters

Weak product visions often sound inspirational but do not help teams make decisions.

Consider the characteristics of visions that fail to guide product work:

- Too broad to distinguish priorities
- Feature-focused rather than outcome-focused
- Disconnected from a real user problem
- Impossible to use for prioritization
- Not meaningfully different from competitors
- No indication of the outcome being pursued

The cost of a weak vision is real. Teams spend energy debating priorities that the vision doesn't clarify. Good ideas get rejected without context. The roadmap becomes reactive rather than intentional.

Here is a useful test: If two completely different roadmaps could both satisfy the same vision, the vision may not be specific enough to guide the product.

## My Approach

I do not start by asking: **"What should our vision statement be?"**

I start by asking:

- Who are we serving?
- What important problem are we solving?
- What should be meaningfully different for them in the future?
- Why are we positioned to create that future?
- What evidence would tell us we are getting closer?

The vision statement is the compression of those decisions — not the starting point.

## The Framework

**MY PRODUCT VISION FRAMEWORK**

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ WHO                                         │
│ Who is the primary user or customer whose  │
│ problem matters most?                      │
│                                             │
│               ↓                             │
│                                             │
│ PROBLEM                                     │
│ What meaningful unmet need, friction,       │
│ or constraint are we trying to change?     │
│                                             │
│               ↓                             │
│                                             │
│ FUTURE STATE                                │
│ What should become possible or materially  │
│ better if we succeed?                      │
│                                             │
│               ↓                             │
│                                             │
│ DIFFERENTIATION                             │
│ Why should this product exist rather than  │
│ simply copying the current market?         │
│                                             │
│               ↓                             │
│                                             │
│ OUTCOME                                     │
│ What observable change would indicate that │
│ we are moving toward the vision?           │
│                                             │
│               ↓                             │
│                                             │
│ VISION STATEMENT                            │
│ The compressed expression of the above     │
│                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Each step builds on the previous one. Skipping a step weakens the foundation.

**WHO** — Avoid serving everyone equally. A primary audience makes the rest of the choices clearer.

**PROBLEM** — The problem must be meaningful enough that solving it matters to the user. If the problem is weak, the vision becomes weak.

**FUTURE STATE** — This describes what becomes better for the user, not how the product will work. "Users can see all their data in one place" is a feature. "Users spend 80% less time managing their information across multiple systems" is a future state.

**DIFFERENTIATION** — Why this product, why this team, why now? This grounds the vision in reality rather than abstract aspiration.

**OUTCOME** — What will change measurably or observably if the vision is working? Without this, there is no way to know if you are moving toward or away from the vision.

## How I Apply It

**Step 1 — Start with evidence, not aspirations**

Review:
- User research and feedback
- Product behavior and usage patterns
- Customer pain points and unmet needs
- Market context and competitive landscape
- Business constraints and resources
- Technical realities and feasibility

**Step 2 — Identify the primary audience**

Avoid trying to make every user equally central. If the vision serves parents, teachers, and students equally, it will likely serve none of them well. Identify who matters most.

**Step 3 — Define the change, not the feature**

The future state should describe what becomes better for the user, not the implementation. The difference matters for making later decisions about technology, design, and priority.

**Step 4 — Test whether the vision helps the team say "no"**

Ask: Would this vision help us choose between two competing roadmap ideas?

If the answer is no, the vision may still be too broad. Refine it until it creates meaningful trade-offs.

**Step 5 — Compress the thinking into clear language**

Only now create the actual vision statement. The statement should be memorable and clear, but it is only valuable because the team understands the thinking underneath it.

## Trade-offs & Common Mistakes

**1. Writing the slogan first**

A memorable sentence without underlying choices is branding, not product direction. It sounds good and fails to guide decisions.

**2. Trying to serve everyone**

A vision becomes weaker when no audience is clearly primary. Each choice must be negotiated rather than followed.

**3. Turning the vision into a feature list**

"AI-powered", "mobile-first", "personalized" describe implementation, not the future the product is trying to create. Implementation changes as constraints change. The future state should remain stable longer.

**4. Making the vision impossible to challenge**

A useful vision should create choices and trade-offs. If every possible roadmap could satisfy the vision, it is not creating clarity.

**5. Confusing vision with strategy**

**Vision** = the future we want to create.

**Strategy** = the choices about how we will move toward it.

**Roadmap** = the sequence of bets we make along the way.

These are different things. Clarity about which is which prevents wasted arguments about product direction.

## Checklist / Key Takeaways

Before I consider a product vision strong enough to guide the team, I ask:

□ Is it clear who we are primarily serving?

□ Is the underlying problem meaningful?

□ Does it describe a better future rather than a list of features?

□ Does it help us distinguish what belongs from what does not?

□ Can it guide prioritization when good ideas compete?

□ Is the vision distinct from the strategy and roadmap?

□ Can we eventually observe whether we are moving toward it?

---

A strong product vision is not valuable because it sounds ambitious. It is valuable because it helps a team make better decisions.
